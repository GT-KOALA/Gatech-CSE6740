# Diffusion Foundations and the Forward Process

These notes cover slides 1–15 of Lecture 16: the limits of autoencoders and VAEs, the overall design of diffusion models, the forward (diffusion) process and its closed form, and noise schedules. Slides 16–27 (the denoising process, the training loss, and sampling) are in Core explanation II.

## Notation

- $$x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x}$$: a clean data sample (e.g., an image flattened into a vector); $$x_t$$: the noisy version after $$t$$ forward steps
- $$T$$: the total number of diffusion steps ($$T = 1000$$ in the slides' plots and in Ho et al., 2020)
- $$\beta_t \in (0, 1)$$: the noise variance added at step $$t$$; the sequence $$\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T$$ is the noise schedule
- $$\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$$ and $$\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s$$: the per-step and cumulative signal factors (the slides use $$\bar\alpha_t$$ without defining $$\alpha_t$$; this is the DDPM convention)
- $$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$: standard Gaussian noise with the same shape as $$x_0$$. The slides write $$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{1}) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x}$$, meaning one independent standard normal per coordinate
- $$q(\cdot)$$: distributions of the forward process, which acts as the encoder

## From VAEs to Diffusion (slides 3–6)

### Recap: AE and VAE

Both models compress an input $$x$$ into a latent code $$z$$ and decode it back to $$x'$$ (slide 4).

- **Autoencoder (AE).** The encoder $$z = q(\cdot \mid x, \phi)$$ and the decoder $$x' = p(\cdot \mid z, \theta)$$ are deterministic neural networks, trained with the reconstruction loss

$$
\min_{\phi, \theta} \; \lVert x - x' \rVert^2 .
$$

- **Variational autoencoder (VAE).** Both maps are stochastic. The encoder predicts a mean and a variance, $$z \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\mu_\phi(x), \sigma^2_\phi(x)\big)$$, and the decoder predicts a mean with a fixed variance, $$x' \sim \mathcal{N}\big(G_\theta(z), \eta I\big)$$. A KL term keeps the code distribution close to a standard Gaussian:

$$
\min_{\phi, \theta} \; \lVert x - x' \rVert^2 + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x, \phi) \,\Vert\, \mathcal{N}(0, I)\big).
$$

The VAE is stochastic because a generative model should produce varied new samples, while a deterministic AE maps the same input to the same output every time. Lecture 15 covers both models in detail.

### What is wrong with VAEs?

Slide 6 encodes $$8 \times 8$$ handwritten digits into a 2-D latent space and decodes them back. The figure shows two problems:

- Codes of different digits overlap near the origin. The KL term pulls every code distribution toward $$\mathcal{N}(0, I)$$, yet the encoded points still differ from an exact unit Gaussian, as the plot's caption notes.
- The reconstructions are blurry. Even at $$8 \times 8$$ pixels, some outputs are hard to recognize.

The blur comes from the decoder. One network has to turn a 2-D code into all 64 pixels in a single pass. For a $$1000 \times 1000$$ image, the same network would have to produce a million pixel values from a tiny code in one forward pass. (In class: unless the decoder is extremely deep and powerful, this one-shot generation is close to impossible.) This is why VAEs and AEs tend to produce low-resolution or blurry images.

Diffusion models generate an image over many small steps. Each step removes a little noise, so the model can refine details gradually and reach much higher image quality.

## Diffusion Models (slides 7–9)

A diffusion model has two processes (slide 9):

1. **Forward diffusion process (fixed).** Gaussian noise is added to a data sample step by step until only noise remains. This process has no learnable parameters.
2. **Reverse denoising process (generative).** Generation starts from random noise and removes noise step by step until a clean sample appears. A neural network learns this direction (Core explanation II).

The slides attribute diffusion models to Ho et al. (2020), whose *Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models* (DDPM) is the formulation this lecture follows. Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015) introduced diffusion probabilistic models earlier, and DDPM showed that the framework can generate high-quality images.

The top-right figure on slide 7 is Figure 2 of the survey by Yang et al. It shows a dog photo destroyed by noise and then regenerated by denoising. In the castle sequence below it, an image emerges from noise over 1 to 40 denoising steps, with the coarse layout first and fine details last.

### Why "an extension of VAEs"?

Slide 7 calls diffusion models an extension of VAEs: the forward process corresponds to the encoder and the reverse process to the decoder. They differ in three ways:

| | VAE | Diffusion model |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Encoder | A learned neural network $$q(z \mid x, \phi)$$ | A fixed noising process $$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$$ with no neural network |
| Latent space | Low-dimensional, $$d' \ll d$$ | Same dimension as the data, $$x_T \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x}$$ |
| Decoder | One network, one step | One network applied for $$T$$ small steps |
{: style="border-spacing: 1.5em 0.3em; margin-left: -1.5em;"}

The "latent" $$x_T$$ has the shape of an image and holds independent Gaussian noise. (In class: it looks like the static on an old untuned TV.) Its distribution is known exactly, $$\mathcal{N}(0, I)$$, so we can sample from it, and that is what makes it usable as a latent space.

## Adding Noise (slides 10–11)

To go from $$x_{t-1}$$ to $$x_t$$, shrink the image slightly and add a little white noise (slide 10):

$$
x_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\, \epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
$$

Repeating this step many times turns any image into noise.

### Why the square roots?

Slide 10 asks why the coefficients are $$\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}$$ and $$\sqrt{\beta_t}$$ instead of $$1 - \beta_t$$ and $$\beta_t$$. The square roots keep the variance constant.

Multiplying a random variable by a constant $$c$$ multiplies its variance by $$c^2$$, and the variances of independent random variables add. Suppose each pixel of $$x_{t-1}$$ has variance $$v$$ and the new noise $$\epsilon_t$$ is independent of $$x_{t-1}$$. Then

$$
\mathrm{Var}(x_t) = (1 - \beta_t)\, v + \beta_t \cdot 1 .
$$

If the data are normalized so that $$v = 1$$, then $$\mathrm{Var}(x_t) = (1 - \beta_t) + \beta_t = 1$$ at every step. For $$v \ne 1$$, the update pulls $$v$$ toward 1. The overall scale of the image stays the same while the share of the variance that comes from the original image shrinks. This property is often called variance preserving.

Without the square roots, the update $$x_t = (1 - \beta)\, x_{t-1} + \beta\, \epsilon_t$$ gives

$$
\mathrm{Var}(x_t) = (1 - \beta)^2 v + \beta^2 ,
$$

whose fixed point is $$v^\ast = \beta / (2 - \beta)$$. With $$\beta = 0.01$$, the variance collapses to about $$0.005$$, so the signal and the noise both shrink toward zero and the endpoint is far from $$\mathcal{N}(0, I)$$. A simulation of 1000 steps gives a variance of about $$0.005$$ without the roots and about $$1.00$$ with them.

Slide 11 shows the effect on a cat photo. As $$\bar\alpha_t$$ (defined below) falls from 1 through 0.98, 0.9, 0.7, 0.4, 0.15, 0.03, and 0.003 to 0, the cat fades into grain and then into unrecognizable noise.

## Formal Definition (slide 12)

Each step is a Gaussian centered at the shrunken previous sample:

$$
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big).
$$

Sampling from this Gaussian is the update on slide 10. Chaining $$T$$ steps gives a Markov chain $$x_0 \to x_1 \to \cdots \to x_T$$, in which each $$x_t$$ depends only on $$x_{t-1}$$. The joint distribution of the trajectory therefore factorizes:

$$
q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}).
$$

(In class: a VAE needs a neural network for its encoder, while in a diffusion model the noising process itself is the encoder.) The only parameters of this encoder are the $$\beta_t$$. They are set before training so that after $$T$$ steps the sample is essentially pure noise:

$$
q(x_T \mid x_0) \approx \mathcal{N}(x_T;\ 0,\ I).
$$

## The Closed Form (slide 13)

### Signal and noise after t steps

The image is multiplied by $$\sqrt{1 - \beta_s}$$ at every step, so after $$t$$ steps the original signal has been scaled by

$$
\sqrt{1-\beta_1} \cdot \sqrt{1-\beta_2} \cdots \sqrt{1-\beta_t} = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \sqrt{\alpha_s} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} .
$$

The total variance stays at 1, so the accumulated noise has variance $$1 - \bar\alpha_t$$. This gives the closed form

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),
$$

or equivalently $$q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\ (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\big)$$, which is Eq. (4) of Ho et al. (2020).

### Derivation

The slide skips one step: a sum of independent Gaussian noises is again Gaussian, with the variances added. The closed form follows by induction on $$t$$.

**Base case.** For $$t = 1$$, $$\bar\alpha_1 = \alpha_1 = 1 - \beta_1$$, and the closed form is the one-step update.

**Inductive step.** Assume $$x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, \bar\epsilon$$ with $$\bar\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$. Substitute this into $$x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\, \epsilon_t$$:

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, x_0 + \underbrace{\sqrt{\alpha_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}\, \bar\epsilon + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\, \epsilon_t}_{\text{sum of two independent Gaussians}} .
$$

The first coefficient is $$\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$$ because $$\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1}$$. The noise $$\epsilon_t$$ is fresh, so it is independent of $$\bar\epsilon$$. Both noise terms are zero-mean Gaussians, so their sum is a zero-mean Gaussian with variance

$$
\alpha_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + (1 - \alpha_t) = 1 - \alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1} = 1 - \bar\alpha_t .
$$

The sum therefore has the same distribution as $$\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon$$ with $$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$, which completes the induction. The argument holds for any $$x_0$$, whatever its variance.

Running 300 steps of the linear schedule one at a time and making a single jump with the closed form give the same mean, variance, and correlation with $$x_0$$, up to sampling error.

### Reading the closed form

- $$\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$$ is the signal amplitude, the fraction of the original image left at step $$t$$.
- $$\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$$ is the noise amplitude.
- The ratio $$\bar\alpha_t / (1 - \bar\alpha_t)$$ is the signal-to-noise ratio. It is very large near $$t = 0$$ and falls toward 0 at $$t = T$$.

Every $$\alpha_s$$ is below 1, so $$\bar\alpha_t$$ decreases monotonically. Once $$\bar\alpha_T \approx 0$$, $$x_T \approx \epsilon$$, and $$q(x_T \mid x_0) \approx \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$ for every starting image.

During training, the closed form produces a noisy sample at any step $$t$$ with one formula, so there is no need to run $$t$$ noising steps. Core explanation II builds the training loss on it.

## Noise Schedules (slide 14)

The noise schedule sets how fast the image turns into noise, and it is a hyperparameter of the model. Slide 14 compares three schedules with $$T = 1000$$:

- **Constant:** $$\beta_t = 0.01$$ for all $$t$$.
- **Linear** (Ho et al., 2020): $$\beta_t$$ increases linearly from $$\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$$ to $$\beta_T = 0.02$$. The slide writes $$\beta^T$$, which should be the subscript $$\beta_T$$.
- **Cosine** (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021): $$\bar\alpha_t$$ is defined directly and $$\beta_t$$ is derived from it,

$$
\bar\alpha_t = \frac{f(t)}{f(0)}, \qquad f(t) = \cos^2\!\left( \frac{t/T + s}{1 + s} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} \right), \qquad \beta_t = \min\!\left(1 - \frac{\bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_{t-1}},\ 0.999\right).
$$

The slide leaves out two details from the original paper. The offset is $$s = 0.008$$, chosen so that $$\sqrt{\beta}$$ at the start is slightly smaller than one pixel bin, $$1/127.5$$; tinier noise at the start made it hard for the network to predict the noise accurately. The clip at $$0.999$$ prevents a singularity near $$t = T$$: $$f(T) = \cos^2(\pi/2) = 0$$, so the last step would otherwise have $$\beta_T = 1$$.

The plots on slide 14 show the signal amplitude $$\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$$ (blue) and the noise amplitude $$\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$$ (orange). Recomputing the three schedules gives the table below. The third column is the step where the signal and noise amplitudes are equal ($$\bar\alpha_t = 0.5$$), and the fourth is the first step where the signal amplitude falls below 0.1. The cosine schedule's last value, 0.999, is the clipped one.

| Schedule | $$\beta_1 \to \beta_T$$ | Signal $$=$$ noise | Signal $$< 0.1$$ | $$\bar\alpha_T$$ |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Constant | $$0.01 \to 0.01$$ | 69 | 459 | $$4.3 \times 10^{-5}$$ |
| Linear | $$10^{-4} \to 0.02$$ | 260 | 674 | $$4.0 \times 10^{-5}$$ |
| Cosine | $$4.1 \times 10^{-5} \to 0.999$$ | 497 | 936 | $$2.4 \times 10^{-9}$$ |
{: style="border-spacing: 1.5em 0.3em; margin-left: -1.5em;"}

- **Constant.** $$\bar\alpha_t = 0.99^t$$, so the signal decays exponentially. Most of the noise arrives early: the image is half noise by step 69, and the remaining 900 or so steps all look close to pure noise. (In class: we want the noise to rise steadily all the way from 0 to 1, without a jump at the start.)
- **Linear.** Small early $$\beta_t$$ slow the destruction, and the crossover moves to step 260. The signal amplitude still falls below 0.1 by step 674, so about the last third of the steps are close to pure noise. Nichol and Dhariwal (2021) found that the end of the linear schedule is too noisy to contribute much to sample quality, especially for $$32 \times 32$$ and $$64 \times 64$$ images.
- **Cosine.** $$\bar\alpha_t$$ changes little near $$t = 0$$ and $$t = T$$ and drops roughly linearly in between. The curves cross near the midpoint (step 497), and the signal amplitude stays above 0.1 until step 936, so only the last 6% or so of the steps are close to pure noise.

The schedule affects training from both ends. If the image becomes pure noise too early, the network sees almost no signal at many timesteps, and those steps teach it little. If noise is added too slowly, $$x_T$$ may still show structure and then does not match the $$\mathcal{N}(0, I)$$ noise used to start generation.

## Examples (slide 15)

Slide 15 animates the forward process on two datasets; the PDF shows only the first frame.

- **2-D toy data.** Eight tight clusters of points form a ring, and each point is one data sample. At every step, each point is shrunk slightly toward the origin and moved by a small random Gaussian step. By step 200, the clusters have merged into a single round Gaussian blob, and the position of a point no longer shows which cluster it came from.
- **MNIST digits.** Handwritten digits 0 to 9 grow grainier until they are pure white noise and no longer recognizable.

In terms of distributions, the forward process turns the data distribution (eight clusters) into the standard Gaussian. The denoising process has to learn the way back, from the Gaussian blob to the eight clusters.

## Classroom Dialog

- **What are the potential issues of VAEs?** One student suggested that the latent distribution might be multimodal instead of Gaussian. This can happen, since the KL term pushes codes toward $$\mathcal{N}(0, I)$$ without forcing an exact match, but it is not the main problem. Another student noticed that the generated $$8 \times 8$$ images look even worse than the inputs. That points to the decoder bottleneck: one network has to produce the whole image from a tiny code in one step.
- **Why the square root in $$\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$$ and $$\sqrt{\beta_t}$$?** Scaling a random variable by $$c$$ scales its variance by $$c^2$$, so with the square roots the two variances add up to 1 and the variance stays fixed over time.
- **Why add noise over many steps if one step could do it?** To produce $$x_t$$, one step is enough: we can jump from $$x_0$$ to any $$x_t$$ by scaling the image and adding a single Gaussian noise. This describes the same process and is the closed form on slide 13. The many small steps matter for the reverse direction, where each denoising step only has to undo a small change.
